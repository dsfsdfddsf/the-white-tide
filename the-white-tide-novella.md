# THE WHITE TIDE
## Chapter 1 — The Early Tide

The water of New Antilla remembers. That is the first thing every pilot learns, and the first thing every child here learns to be afraid of.

Not in the way the old stories sold it — no ghosts, no voices from the deep. Just physics with a grain of ritual mixed in. The Pacific off our latitude runs on a mineral the survey ships found a century ago and the city built its fortune on: the salt lattice, a dissolved isotope that does something no other water does. It holds imprints. Not sounds, not pictures — the whole charge of a person, the way they weighted the world, dissolved at the exact moment of death into still, cold, living water.

If the dead were given the rite.

That is the sentence every pilot repeats before a long watch, the way other ports say their prayers. If the dead were given the rite. Because the water does not decide what to keep. It only keeps what the rite gives it.

I was on the outer ring at 04:12, three weeks before grief season, when I found the white channel.

You feel a normal channel the way you feel a fever on your skin — a pressure that is not pressure, a presence that has weather. The old ones, the ones that have been drifting for years, taste cold and slow, like remembering a word you stopped using. The new ones, the ones the rite put down last month, run hot and fast, almost loud. You pilot around them the way you pilot around anything in the outer lanes: respect the current, keep your bow off the dense stuff, and never listen twice to the same imprint. Twice is how the water gets into your own head.

The white channel was none of that.

It was the absence of a channel. A shape in the current where the current should have been — a river inside the river that carried nothing. I had flown harbor water for four years and there is no word in the pilot's ledger for what I felt, because there has never been anything to feel it about. The listening rod went dead in my hand the way a compass goes dead over an empty sea. Not quiet. Empty.

"Logging a dead channel at the ring," I said to the watchroom, which is what you say when you do not have a name for something and you intend to get one.

The watchroom logged it. The watchroom does not ask questions. The watchroom is the most polite institution in New Antilla.

I should have left it there. That is the rule of the outer lanes: log, mark, keep your distance, and let grief season do its thing. Grief season is when the tide turns and pulls every channel in the open water toward the platform, and the city does the listening — three days of public remembrance in the great hall, the rods on every stand, the water given its day of voice. It is the one time a year we let the dead say whatever they left in the sea.

Grief season is November. It was October.

I turned the little boat into the white and I will not tell you it was brave, because it was not. It was the thing a pilot does when the current is lying to them. You go where the current should be and you see what is holding it open.

It held me for forty minutes.

Forty minutes of nothing, in a sea that is never nothing. The water around the white ran over it the way a river runs over a stone, and I could have been standing on the back of something huge. The rod stayed dead. The dead did not speak through it, which was the new part. The dead do not have to speak through the rod — the rod is for the living to hear with — but the water always, always hummed where a channel lived. A channel is a place where someone was. This was a place where someone had been and the sea had refused to hold the shape of it.

And then the rod woke up, and it was not the sea talking.

It was a voice.

Not through the rod, not in the register of the hum. A voice the way you hear a voice in a crowded room before you see the person — direct, dry, no water in it at all.

"Ruya."

My sister's name in my sister's voice, out of the Pacific, three weeks before the dead are allowed to have a day.

I know how it sounds. I know that Miren has been dead for three years, drowned in the Antilla II wreck in the same water I was standing in, given no rite because there was no body to give the rite to, the unreturned, the ones the city puts in the ledger under a column with no name. I know that the water only keeps what the rite gives it, and that she was never given to it, and that this is the first time in the history of the city that anything has ever come back from the water without a rite.

I know all of it.

I reached for the rod with both hands and the sea around the white went very still, the way a sea goes still when it is about to say something it has been holding for three years.

"Miren?" I said.

The stillness broke. The white took the shape of a current again, fast and hot and new, the way a channel runs the month it is put down, and the rod began to hum with a sound I have only ever heard once before, in the great hall, on a grief season night, in a register so low you feel it in your teeth.

The register of the unreturned.

And under the hum, dry and direct, no water in it at all:

"The water is keeping count now. Come see what we are."

---

The ledger of New Antilla is two things: the ship's ledger, which the harbor keeps, and the rite ledger, which the city keeps, and the rite ledger is the only document in the city that more people sign than read. Every death, every rite, every channel the water has been given in a century. I pulled the Antilla II page off the archive wall at 06:00, because the page for a wreck is the one page of the rite ledger that is not a list of names. It is a number.

4,112.

The Antilla II was the city's second platform, forty years old when it went down in one night, in a storm that the meteorologists still would not explain. 4,112 people aboard. The salvage recovered what it recovered. The rite was done at sea, at dawn, on the water, over the place, in the oldest form of it, the form that has to be spoken by a pilot because a pilot is the only person in the city the water will not refuse.

I had read that the rite had been done for all 4,112, because that was what the city said, and the city does not lie about the water. The city lies about everything else, in its way, politely, at scale.

But the ledger page for the Antilla II is not a list of names. I have been a pilot for four years and I have just now understood why. It is a number, because a number is what you write down when you have 4,112 people and you did the rite for 600 of them, and the other 3,512 you put in the column with no name, because the column with no name is where the city puts what it will not grieve.

Miren was in the column with no name.

I have known, for three years, that the city does not count her the way it counts the rest of the dead. I have not known that the city keeps a page for her. I have not known that the number on the page is a wall, and that I am standing on the inside of it, and that the water, for the first time in a century, has started to lean through it.

The tide table in the watchroom is a wall of brass, forty years of the city's best tides, the one instrument we trust more than the meteorologists. I ran my hand down it this morning, on the way to the great hall, and I found, in the bottom row, in the column for the month the Antilla II went down, a notation in ink that is not brass:

WHITE. 3,512. INBOUND.

Nobody has signed it. The ink is new.

## Chapter 2 — The Rules of the Water

Teo was waiting at the watch table, which was not where a man of his age went at 06:30, and not with a face he wears at any hour.

"You heard it," he said. It was not a question.

"You know what it is."

"I know what it is not." He set his tea down the way a man sets down a verdict. "Sit. I will give you the water's rules the way the water gives them. Five. And I will tell you which one you have broken, even though you did not know the rule when you broke it."

The five rules of New Antilla are taught to every pilot before the rod, and I had them in my head the way you have your own heartbeat, until I heard him say them as if they were incantations:

"First. The water imprints only what is returned to it. Dawn, still water, cold, at the moment of death, or not at all. The rite is the only door in."

"Second. An imprint does not speak. It echoes to the listener. The rod is the ear of the living; what you hear is the imprint bent through your own hearing. Two listeners stand in the same channel and hear different things, and neither is wrong. That is why we never listen twice. The water does not record what you heard; it records who you were when you heard it."

"Third. The tide carries the channel. Grief season is when the current turns the dead toward the city, and the city keeps the water's day of voice. We pilot around it the other eleven months."

"Fourth. The imprint fades. A channel lives about three years, then the charge goes and only the weather is left: a faint current, no content. The old dead are the calm water of the city. That is why we fear the new dead, not the old."

"And the fifth," he said, "you have already broken."

"Fifth," Ruya repeated.

"Whatever was not returned is not in the water. It does not fade, because it does not accumulate. It is not a thing. It is a column with no name, and the column with no name is empty. That is the rule, and that is the reason your sister is not in the sea, and that is the reason the sea owed you three years of silence."

Teo's hands were steady. His voice was not.

"Then what was that?"

"A century ago, nobody would have answered you."

---

The council met at dusk, in the hall above the great stand, where the rods in the walls stay warm all year. Councillor Doss ran it the way he runs everything: short, polite, and closed.

"Storm noise," he said, of the white. "A lattice bloom. The survey ships report a warm layer off the ring this season; the current is doing what warm water does. We will wait for the season, as we have always waited for the season. The water has eleven weeks, and the city has a city to run."

The pilots sat in the second tier, because the pilots are the hands of the city and not its head. Young Rhea, who has two years on the water and none of the patience they give you with years, asked the question in the way young pilots ask it, flat and without respect:

"Councillor. If the white is a channel, the ledger will tell us what it is. The rite ledger is the city's own book."

"The rite ledger is the city's document," Doss said. "And the city's document is not the harbor's evidence. The matter is closed for the season."

The matter closed the way a door closes in a house where someone is holding it open from the other side.

---

I went back to the white that afternoon, because a pilot does not leave a current lying, and because Teo had said *a door* and I am not the kind of person who can put that down.

The rules say you do not test the water. Pilots observe; the city tests. I was on watch, and watch is a word that covers a great deal, and I took the little boat out alone to the edge of the white, and I held the rod still in both hands the way you hold a bird, and I did the thing the rules have no name for, because the rules are all about what the dead give the water, and nothing is written about what the living give it.

I said my own name.

"Ruya."

The water took it the way still water takes a stone, and then it gave it back. Not Miren's voice. Mine. Dry, no water in it at all, coming up through the rod like a line read back from a book. I said it again, slower, and the water read it back slower, the way a student reads.

I said, "Miren."

The water said, "Miren."

The rules say an imprint is bent through the listener; the white does the opposite of the rules. The white is a mirror with the glass turned out. It does not hold anyone. It holds whatever stands in it, and it is standing in it now, and it has been standing in it, I began to understand, for three years, and it is not empty the way the ledger says the column with no name is empty. It is empty the way a locked room is empty.

---

The tide table does not lie. It is brass, and it is the one instrument the city trusts more than the meteorologists, and at 02:00, on the watch where I was not supposed to be looking, I watched the white cross two kilometers of open water in a single night.

It had been moving forty meters a day for a month. The weather of the white does not accelerate. Warm water does not accelerate. Nothing in the outer lanes accelerates, because the outer lanes are the city's patient instrument, and patience is the only property of a current you can build a life on.

The white was no longer patient.

And in the far register, the register you feel in your teeth, the one the city listens to only in the great hall and only in November, the rod held a sound all night that I will write down once, because if I write it down twice I am not a pilot, I am a witness:

my own name, dry, no water in it at all, said by something in the sea that has had three years to practice saying it.

Teo found me at the rail at dawn, and he did not ask how I was, and he did not look at the table, and he said the sentence that is going to be the hardest thing I have to answer, because it is not a question, it is a hand:

"Do not speak to it again, Ruya. It is learning the shape of your voice. And a white that knows your voice is not a white anymore."

"It is a door."

## Chapter 3 — The Ledger Room

The ledger room is below the great stand, where the rods in the walls stay warm, and it is the one room in New Antilla that the city locks without saying so. You do not need a key; you need to be a person the city has decided to let near its own memory.

Teo did not let me near it, and then, two days after the watch, he walked me into it anyway, because the sea had moved again, and because a pilot who is losing the sea will take whatever hand the city still has.

"Look," he said, at the shelf. "Not at the Antilla II. The page before it."

I looked at the page before it, and then I understood the shape of the thing I was standing in.

The rite ledger is not one book. It is a century of books, one per year, and each year is a column: the named, the returned, the cost. Before the Antilla II, the last column in every book was empty, and the column had no name, and the column with no name was empty, because a city that can afford its dead does not need a column for the ones it cannot.

The Antilla II book was different. The named column held 600. The returned column held 600. And the column with no name, which had stood empty for a century, held 3,512.

"Who filled it?" I asked.

"Who do you think fills a column?" Teo said.

---

The rule of the rite is not only what I was taught. I have flown harbor water for four years, and four years is how long it takes to learn that the city's five rules are the city's five doors, and that behind every door there is a door, and behind that door the bill.

The rite costs. This is not taught, because it is not new. The pilot who speaks the rite takes the moment of death into the season: one dawn in still water, one night a year on the outer ring, the listener standing where the dead stand, so that the imprint goes into the water clean and not through a witness. It is called the season of the witness, and every pilot in the city takes it, and the city has always had enough pilots to take it, because the city has always had a manageable number of dead.

4,112 is not a manageable number of dead.

The city did not have the pilots. The city did not have the dawn. The city had one storm, one wreck, and a ledger, and it did the arithmetic the way the city does everything: politely, at scale, and with the numbers that looked right at the time. 600 named. 600 returned. The rest into the column, and the column closed, and the column with the name on it was a wall, and the wall was the city's way of paying its dead in full, for a hundred years.

The arithmetic does not age well. The arithmetic is the white.

---

I found Miren in the ledger on the third night, and I will not tell you how I found her, because the way I found her is the part I cannot put in the book, and you can read about everything else in this city and not read about that part.

Her name is not in the 600. Her name is not in the column, because the column holds no names; the column holds the count of the names the city will not say, and the city did not say her name, because her name was the name of the man who signed the 600, and the man who signs the 600 is not a number the city puts in a column.

I stood in the ledger room with the page open and the two columns facing each other, the 600 on the left and the 3,512 on the right, and I understood that my sister had not drowned the way I was told she drowned, and that the city had not buried her at sea with full rite the way her grave stone says, and that the grave stone is a wall, and that I have been standing on the inside of her for three years and calling it grief, when it was the city's column, keeping its count.

Teo found me there. He did not look at the page. He looked at the column.

"You were not in the 600," he said.

"No."

"The city told your mother full rite."

"The city tells a great deal."

"The city told her, because your mother was a pilot, and a pilot who learns her daughter was in the column with no name stops flying for the city, and the city was four months short of pilots in the year after the wreck, and the city does not let a debt it cannot collect sit open, so the city collected the lie instead."

He said it the way the water says it: without water in it at all.

"And the 600," I said. "The city chose the 600 because the bodies were found."

"The city chose the 600," Teo said, "because the rite costs, and the city could afford the cost of the 600, and the cost of the 3,512 would have been the whole city, and the city is not a person who can stand the whole city, so the city stood what it could stand, and the rest went into the column, and the column has been holding the count for three years, and the water has been holding the shape of the count, and the shape of the count is what you stood in on the ring, Ruya, three weeks before the dead are allowed to have a day."

"I did not break a rule," I said. "I broke a bill."

"The water does not distinguish," Teo said. "The water is the only honest instrument in the city. You cannot close a column without paying it. The city closed the column. The water has come to collect."

## Chapter 4 — The White Water Speaks

The city does not stop the sea with words. It stops the sea with walls.

The old tide-walls sit in the outer ring like the teeth of a machine the city built to cut channels, and the council voted them at 06:00, in the hall, in the warm rods, with the sea two kilometers off the platform and accelerating at a rate the weather does not accelerate at.

"Storm noise," Doss said, for the last time, in the way a man says a thing he has stopped believing. "The walls will cut the bloom. The season will do its work."

The walls were built for the dead. I stood in the second tier and watched the city vote to cut a thing that was not the dead, and I understood that the city does not know what it is voting on, because the city is the one that closed the column, and a city cannot read the bill it has refused to open.

---

I went back to the white that night, because the rules have no name for the thing I was about to do, and because Teo had said *a door*, and a door is not a thing you vote on; a door is a thing you stand in.

The water took my voice the way it had taken it on the first night, and gave it back the way it had, dry and without water in it at all, and I stood in the little boat at the edge of the white and I did not say my name, because I had learned what my name was worth, and I said the only thing left that was not the city's:

"Who are you?"

And the white answered in a voice that was not mine.

Not Miren's voice. Rhea's voice, flat and without respect, the way young pilots say it: "I am the one who asked the question in the hall." And under it, the watch-officer's voice, the one who had not asked the question, because he had not been in the hall, because the watch-officer is not in the second tier, and the watch-officer had stood on the ring at 02:00 with a rod and had not said a word, and the white was giving him back the shape of the word he had not said.

The white is not a channel of the 3,512. The white is a mirror the city has stopped watching, and a mirror, left unwatched, begins to hold whatever stands in it, and the city has been standing in it, the whole city, on the outer ring, on the watches, in the hall, with the rods warm in the walls, for three weeks, and the white is holding the city the way the city held the column: politely, at scale, and without a name on it.

The living do not fade. This is the fourth rule, and the white is the fourth rule run backwards, and the city has been paying the column with the living for a month, and the bill is no longer 3,512, and the bill is not going to stop being 3,512, because a mirror that holds the living is not a debt the city signed; it is a debt the city is signing every watch, in the warm rods, with the whole of the city standing in it, and no one in the city knows the shape of the signature.

---

Teo came to me on the rail at dawn, and he did not say *I told you not to*, because a man who has carried 600 moments of death for three years does not have the shape of a man who says that, and he set his two hands flat on the rail, the way the water sets a verdict down, and he said the sentence I have been waiting for this whole time to hear, because the sentence is not a confession, the sentence is the bill:

"I signed the ledger, Ruya. I chose the 600, because the rite costs, and the city could afford the cost of the 600, and I have carried the season of the witness since the wreck, longer than any pilot in the city, and I have not slept three years, because a pilot who speaks the rite takes the moment of death into the season, and I took 600 moments of death into one season, and the season is not over, and the season will not be over until the water has the 3,512, and the water has come to collect in person, and it is no longer collecting with the tide, it is collecting with the city, and the city is the white now, and the white is the city, and I am the last person in New Antilla who knows the shape of the bill, and I will not sign it a second time, and you are the pilot, and the pilot is the only one the water will not refuse, and I am giving you the rod."

He set the rod on the rail. The rod is the one instrument in the city that the water does not refuse, and the water has been refusing the city for three weeks, and the rod on the rail was the shape of a key the city had not known it was holding.

---

The walls cut at dawn. I will not tell you they cut clean, because they did not, and I will not tell you what the white did when the walls cut it, because what the white did is the chapter you are about to read, and the city is watching the walls, and the city is not watching the white, and the white is watching the city, and the white has been patient for three years, and the white is no longer patient, and the white is no longer coming toward the platform.

It is waiting for the city to come.

# THE WHITE TIDE
## Chapter 5 — The Return

The walls did not cut the white. The walls did what walls do, which is stop the thing they were built to stop, and the white is not the thing the walls were built to stop, and the white went through the teeth of the machine the way water goes through a wall that is also water, and the city watched, in the warm rods of the hall, the white scatter under the platform.

The lattice runs under the city. This is the thing the city built its fortune on and the thing the city drinks, and the rods in the walls of the great stand are the city's own rods, the warm ones, and the white went under them, and the city's water went the color of the column with no name, and Councillor Doss, who does not lose his composure, lost his, because a city can close a ledger and live, and a city cannot close a ledger under its own floor.

"Seal the ring," he said. "The city will stand on what it has. The season will do its work."

The city voted to stand on the bill. The city has always stood on the bill.

I was on the outer ring with the rod, because the rod is the one instrument in the city that the water does not refuse, and the water had just refused the city for three weeks, and the water had been waiting, and the water was no longer patient, and the water does not wait for the city; the water waits for the pilot.

---

The plan is not mine. The plan is the rite, and the rite is a hundred years old, and the rite is not one thing, and the rite is three things: the dead are given to the water, the water is given to the city, and the city is given to the witness. The city had been paying the first two and not the third, for a century, because the third is the one the city does not want, and the third is the one that costs, and the third is the shape of the bill.

I towed the listening array into the white at dawn, the way you tow anything into the thing it is not built for, and the array ran the length of the city's stands, the warm rods, every stand in New Antilla, and I towed it into the white the way you tow a line into the current that is not built for a line, and the white took the array the way the white takes everything, and the array began to hum, and the hum went up the line, and the hum went into the warm rods, and the warm rods went into the city, and the city, which had been standing on the bill, began, for the first time in three weeks, to hear the shape of it.

The rite is not one voice. The rite is the witness, and the witness is the living, and the living are the ones who stand in the white, and I stood in the white with the array in my hands and I spoke the rite the way the water speaks it, without water in it at all:

"Who are you?"

And the city, in the warm rods, in every stand, in the lattice under the floor, answered the way a city answers when it has stood in the mirror long enough:

"We are the ones who asked the question. We are the ones who did not say the word. We are the ones who closed the column. We are the ones who have been standing in it since the wreck."

And I gave the city back to itself, the way you give a mirror back to the face it has been holding:

"You are alive. The word is yours. The white is not yours. The white is the 3,512, and the 3,512 are not the city, and the city does not hold them, and the city will not hold them again, and the city will give them the rite, the way the city gives the water the dead, the way the water gives the city the witness, the way the witness gives the dead the name, and the name will not be a number, and the number will not be a wall, and the wall will not be the city's way of paying the dead in full, because the city cannot pay the dead in full, and the city can pay the dead their names."

---

Miren's voice came up through the array at the end of the rite, and it was not an echo, and it was not the register of the hum, and it was not the dry, no water in it at all of the first night, and it was not a word I had said, and it was not a word the city had said, and it was a word Miren never said, because Miren has been dead for three years, and a dead woman does not say new words, and the word was the shape of the bill, and the word was the only word the water had been holding for three years, the word the 3,512 had been waiting for, the word the city had closed the column to avoid:

"The water keeps count."

And under it, dry and without water in it at all, the second half of the first night, the half the city had not heard, because the city was not standing in the white yet, because the city was the white yet:

"Come see what we are."

The white took the shape of a current again, and the current ran cold and slow, the weather of a channel three years old, the way the old dead run, the way the calm water of the city runs, and the 3,512 were in the water, and the water was holding them the way the water holds the dead, and the dead were not the city anymore, and the city was not the dead, and the bill was open, and the bill was being paid, and the pilot who tows the line into the current that is not built for a line is the only one the water does not refuse.

The white settled. The white began, for the first time in three years, to fade.

# THE WHITE TIDE
## Chapter 6 — The New Rite

The city does not change the way the city changes. The city changes the way water changes: a degree at a time, over a season, until the shape of the thing is not the shape it was, and no one can say the hour it changed, and everyone can say it changed, and the change is in the water, and the water is the city, and the city is the one who signs the bill.

The rite of the witness was written into the ledger on the first day of grief season, three weeks after the white settled, and the ledger page is not a number anymore, and the column with no name holds no names, because the city opened the wreck manifest, and the wreck manifest held 4,112 names, and the names went into the ledger the way the names go into the water: one by one, in the cold, at dawn, by the witness who stands in the white and says the name out loud, and the name goes into the water, and the name is not a number, and the number is not a wall.

The city learned the third thing of the rite in one season, the thing it had not paid for a hundred years, and the third thing is the cheapest thing in the rite and the only thing the city refused, because the third thing is the witness, and the witness is the living, and the living are the ones who stand in the white, and the living are the ones who do not fade, and the living give the dead the name, and the name is the only payment the water accepts that does not cost the city its own memory.

Grief is not metered anymore. This is the new rule, and the new rule has no number on it, because a rule with a number on it is the old rule, and the old rule is the bill. The city meters nothing, and the city does not stand on the bill, and the city stands in the white, the whole city, on the warm rods, in the great stand, on a grief season night, and the water says whatever it left in the sea, and the city listens, and the city does not close the column, because the column is open, and the open column is the only column the water will not lean through.

---

Teo slept on the first night of the new rite, and the city will not tell you how long he slept, because Teo is not the city's instrument anymore, and Teo is the man who carried 600 moments of death into one season, and the season is over, and the season was the shape of the bill, and the bill is open, and the man who signs the bill does not carry the bill, and Teo is the first man in New Antilla to put the rod down and sleep, and the city watched him sleep the way the city watches the water, with the respect of the instrument, because a man who has carried the dead for three years and put them down is the closest thing the city has to a saint, and the city does not have saints, the city has pilots, and the pilots are the hands of the city, and the hands of the city, finally, are allowed to rest.

I am the witness pilot. This is the rank the city gave me, and the rank has no number on it, and the rank is the one who tows the line into the current that is not built for a line, and the rank is the one the water does not refuse, and the rank is the one who stands in the white and says the name out loud, and the name goes into the water, and the water holds the name the way the water holds the dead, and the dead are not the city anymore, and the city is not the dead.
